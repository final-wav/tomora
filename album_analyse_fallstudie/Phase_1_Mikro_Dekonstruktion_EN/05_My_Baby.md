# Track 05: "My Baby"
## The Construction of a Hermetic Dyadic Cult: Linguistic Regression, the Grammar of the Monad, and the Manic Rocking Motion of Autohypnosis

---

### I. Album Dramaturgy: Context & Threshold Function

Within the architectural framework of *TOMORA*, "My Baby" occupies the most precarious and deceitful threshold position of the album's entire first half. To grasp the existential necessity of this track, one must survey the landscape of wreckage left behind by its immediate predecessor: in Track 04 ("Ring The Alarm"), the album culminated in a catastrophic meltdown of the neurovegetative system. The hesitant desire of "Please", the territorial claim of incorporation in "Come Closer", and the paranoid cognitive bid for omnipotence in "A Boy Like You" ("I know you / You don’t know me") had all collapsed there in a sensory barrage of sirens. Above all, Track 04 had pronounced a verdict of total epistemic bankruptcy in its bridge: the ego screamed its repetitive agony—"Don't know why I do it / I don't know"—into overdrive distortion, thereby confessing that all conscious agency and behavioral autonomy had shattered.

"My Baby" is the immediate, panic-stricken counter-reaction to this cognitive and somatic state of emergency. Yet the psyche, incapable of enduring the devastation of the alarm, undergoes neither dialectical clarification nor a therapeutic process of mourning—instead, it flees into a padded capsule, an artificially hermetic protective bubble. The track relates to "Ring The Alarm" as dissociative anesthesia relates to traumatic shock. Where deafening noise, sirens, and loss of control previously reigned, the soundscape now flips into a seductively supple, buoyant, almost music-box-like idiom.

Yet this pastoral tranquility is a neurotic decoy. The ego does not process the crisis; rather, it evades it through a radical, autosuggestive regression: it invents a hermetic dyadic universe, a *folie à deux*, in which the threatening outside world is sealed off completely. In place of forfeited knowledge ("don't know"), there emerges not authentic insight, but a circular, unquestionable surrogate for evidentiary truth: pure, autopoietic feeling ("I feel it"). The song stages the desperate maneuver of a subject that refuses with all its might to acknowledge the failure of attachment, rocking itself instead into a repetitive swaying motion. In doing so, Track 05 inevitably paves the way for the radical isolation of Track 06 ("Have You Seen Me Dance Alone?"): when the fantasized dyadic monad suffocates on its own inner void, the psyche's only remaining recourse in the subsequent step is absolute solipsism.

### III. The Exhaustive Reading: 27-Point Deep Dive

#### 1. The Padded Capsule After the Emergency: Flight into a Pseudo-Oasis
The song begins abruptly with a prefabricated formula. Following the acoustic barrage and siren-induced panic of "Ring The Alarm", the subject undergoes no gradual convalescence, but plunges headlong into a pre-constructed linguistic dwelling. Entering this song marks an abrupt phase transition: from an open blaze into an incubator. The psyche chooses not healing, but sedating anesthesia. The coziness of the arrangement is suspicious from its very first second, because it does not integrate the catastrophe of the preceding minute, but overwrites it through sheer repression.

#### 2. The Intertextual Transplant: Nina Simone / Gus Kahn as Psychic Armor
The title line "My baby just cares for me" is not an original thought generated by the lyrical ego, but a direct quotation from one of the most prominent jazz and pop standards of the twentieth century (written in 1928 by Gus Kahn and Walter Donaldson for the musical *Whoopee!*, and iconically immortalized in 1958 by Nina Simone). Yet while the historical original thrives on carefree grandezza, worldly wit, and playful sovereignty—there, the protagonist rejects luxury goods, Cadillacs, and high-fashion gowns because her lover loves her—the formula here is violently torn from its narrative context and repurposed as a frantic shield. The citation functions as a foreign graft: because the ego's own language shattered in Track 04 ("Don't know why I do it"), it borrows a historical, collectively validated phrase to stave off its own impending psychological collapse.

#### 3. "Baby" as Depersonalization: The Counterpart as Transitional Object
Designating the partner as "baby" is a fossilized pop music convention, yet within the psychoanalytic space of *TOMORA*, it acquires a pathological sharpness. The counterpart possesses neither a name nor a contour, neither a history nor a desire of their own. They are infantilized as "baby" and simultaneously degraded into a pure transitional object in the sense of Donald Winnicott: they are not a real, resistant "Thou", but a psychological stuffed animal, an inanimate screen for projection whose sole purpose is to ward off the primal terror of abandonment.

#### 4. The Semantics of Moderation: "Cares" Instead of "Loves" as the Psyche's Minimal Program
Striking here is the semantic modesty of the predicate. The text does not dare assert: "My baby loves me", "My baby worships me", or "My baby needs me". It resorts to the far weaker, almost bureaucratically detached "cares for me". Care (*Fürsorge*) is the lowest common denominator of interpersonal devotion; it vacillates between tender nurturing and custodial obligation. By downsizing desire to mere care, the ego executes an emergency landing: expectations placed upon the other are minimized so drastically that they can scarcely fail to meet them. Whoever is sick, feeble, or traumatized no longer demands ecstasy—they settle for someone keeping vigil by the bedside and not letting them die.

#### 5. The Janus Face of the Particle "Just": Exclusivity Versus Scarcity
The adverb "just" harbors a fundamental ambiguity that exposes the song's entire defensive architecture:
* **The reading of exclusivity:** "Just" in the sense of *solely / exclusively*. No one else matters; their entire attention belongs to me; all other women, all worldly distractions have been eliminated. This is the heroic-romantic defensive assertion.
* **The reading of lack:** "Just" in the sense of *merely / only*. He does *only* the bare minimum. He does not love me passionately, he hardly touches me anymore, but at least he "takes care." The particle involuntarily betrays that the relationship has deteriorated into emergency palliative care. The psyche seeks to assert exclusivity, yet ends up documenting destitution.

#### 6. The Fourfold Hammering in the Intro: The Ritual of Autosuggestion
Four consecutive times in the intro, the ego chants the identical complete formula: "My baby just cares for me". This is no report directed toward an external listener, but the rhythmic recitation of an apotropaic prayer. In psychodynamics, this insistence corresponds to the repetition compulsion: an assertion does not become truer through repetition, but the autonomic nervous system is artificially down-regulated by the monotony of the syllabic sequence. The repetition is designed to numb the brain before doubt can take hold.

#### 7. The First Syntactic Fracture: "For me, for me" — The Disappearance of the Subject
After the fourth cycle, the complete sentence structure (*subject – predicate – object*) shatters. The subject (*my baby*) and the action (*cares*) abruptly drop away. What remains is solely the prepositional directional vector: "For me, for me". This ellipsis is a profoundly telling Freudian slip of form (parapraxis): the apparent agent of devotion (the *baby*) is entirely interchangeable for the ego. What truly matters is greedy, passive reception. The phrase unmasks the narcissistic center of gravity of the song: it is never about the other, but exclusively about one's own addressing.

#### 8. The Isolated Third "For me": Phonetic Lingering in the Vacuum
The doubled "For me, for me" is followed by its own, metrically isolated line: a third, naked "For me". Vocally, this word is no longer propelled forward, but breathed into the room like a sigh. It marks a moment of suspension in which the system senses, for the fraction of a heartbeat, that the directional vector points into emptiness. Without an acting subject, the preposition degenerates into a one-way street: devotion that has no sender.

#### 9. The Chorus as Cramped Re-Establishment of the Façade
In the first chorus, the ego immediately reaches back for the intact baseline formula: three times in succession, "My baby just cares for me" is reconstructed. This return to the full form demonstrates the psychic fragility at play: no sooner does syntax threaten to collapse in the intro than the wall must be hastily replastered. The ego forces itself into composure, smooths out the vocal register, and intones the formula with an almost mechanical cheerfulness.

#### 10. The Apocopated Amputation: "My baby just cares for" — Falling Silent Before the Self
In the fourth line of the chorus, the central structural accident of the song occurs: "My baby just cares for". The sentence breaks off abruptly mid-measure, immediately preceding the pronoun "me". The apocope acts as a traumatic incision. At the most delicate juncture—precisely where the ego ought to be affirmed as a beloved object—phonation fails. For a fraction of a second, an abyss opens: *What if he doesn't care for me at all? What if "cares for" clutches into bottomless nothingness?* Breath catches in the larynx; the music hovers in an unresolved harmonic suspension.

#### 11. The Second Regressive Stage: The Naked "Me, Me / Me, Me" in the Post-Chorus
The response to this moment of shock is not reflection, but a dramatic regression into pre-linguistic space. In the post-chorus, the ego discharges the isolated syllable four times: "Me, Me / Me, Me". Every grammatical embedding is obliterated: the subject is gone, the verb is gone, even the mediating preposition "for" has been blown away. What remains is the raw, unarticulated personal pronoun in the objective case. This is the language of an infant in a crib who possesses no sentences, but points with pounding fists and cries toward its own chest: *Me! Mine! Me!* It is the somatic exposure of absolute lack.

#### 12. The Relational Void: The Radical Absence of "We" and "Us" in the Refrain
It is of fundamental diagnostic significance what is entirely absent from the text's first three sections (Intro, Chorus, Post-Chorus): there is no "we", no "us", no "our". The text claims a love relationship, yet strictly refuses to articulate a shared "we". There exists solely the rigid dichotomy between a distant third person ("my baby") and a needy first-person object ("me"). There is no encounter, no reciprocity, and no exchange—only a vertical, unilateral relation of dependence.

#### 13. The Epistemic Circular Argument: "I feel it, I feel it" as a Surrogate for Truth
Following the breakdown in the post-chorus, the song abruptly shifts to a new register of assertion: "I feel it, I feel it / I feel it, I feel it". Here, the ego executes the classic circular logic of neurosis: because there is no rational proof for the other's care—indeed, because language has just collapsed in front of the proof—the subject retreats into the impregnable fortress of pure subjectivity. "I feel it, after all!" Feeling is elevated to the absolute criterion of truth. One who feels requires no arguments; feeling immunizes itself against any objection from reality.

#### 14. The Doubling of "I feel it": Autohypnosis Against Sensory Numbness
Chanting "I feel it" four times fulfills a dual neurobiological function. On the one hand, it is meant to silence the intellect. On the other, it represents the desperate self-examination of a subject terrified of sensory freezing after the numbness and sensory overstimulation of Track 04. Like someone pinching their own cheek after an accident to test whether nerve pathways are still conducting, the voice reassures itself of its own vitality. Singing "I feel it" is not an expression of abundance, but an incantation against an impending internal vacuum.

#### 15. The Empty Pronoun "it": The Unnameable Object of Sensation
What exactly does the ego actually feel? The text stubbornly refuses to name the object of feeling. It does not say "I feel his love", nor "I feel peace", nor "I feel the touch". It merely says "it". This "it" is a semantic void, a placeholder capable of being charged with any arbitrary illusion. It is the pure, contentless arousal of the nervous system, which must retrospectively be interpreted as "love" so as not to be unmasked as panic.

#### 16. Entering the Compulsive Loop: "Round and round in circles we go"
With the bridge, the apparent static quality of the song breaks open and pivots into rotational motion: "Round and round in circles we go". The metaphor of the circle is the universal emblem of impasse and the repetition compulsion. A circle possesses no beginning, no destination, and no development; every movement inevitably leads back to the identical point of departure. What is staged as a dance or a carousel ride is in truth an imprisoned circling within one's own symptom.

#### 17. The First Appearance of "we": Fellowship Exclusively in Vertigo
In this precise line—and exclusively here—the first-person plural personal pronoun enters the stage for the first time in the entire song: "we go". Yet this mutuality is tethered to a terrible condition: the "we" does not constitute itself through dialogue, through eye contact, or through shared future-building, but solely and exclusively in shared dizziness, in collective vertigo ("in circles we go"). The couple exists only as a coalition of vertigo, spinning together to avoid collapsing.

#### 18. Somatic Rocking Motion: Jactatio Corporis as Soothing Trauma
The rhythmic structure of the bridge—the swaying, repetitive "Round and round"—mimics a fundamental clinical somatic phenomenon: **jactatio corporis** (the stereotypical, monotonous rocking of the torso back and forth). In developmental psychology and trauma therapy, this represents the classic self-soothing behavior of neglected or institutionalized infants who, in the absence of real maternal attunement, rhythmically rock their own bodies to dampen sensory overload and hallucinate baseline soothing. Tom Rowlands' buoyant beat and Aurora's swaying phonation transform the song here into a gargantuan musical cradle intended to rock the traumatized nervous system to sleep.

#### 19. The Grammar of the Monad: The Morphosyntactic Parapraxis "Only me and my baby knows"
In the subsequent line, the most fascinating linguistic phenomenon of the entire work takes place:
> *"Only me and my baby knows"*

Linguistically and grammatically, this sentence is a fundamental parapraxis:
1. **Object pronoun as subject:** In place of the grammatically correct subject pronoun "I", the ego places the objective pronoun "me" at the head of the clause ("Only me..."). The ego experiences itself not as an active agent, but as an object.
2. **The morphological singular rupture (*"knows"*):** Since two persons are involved (*me* and *my baby*), the predicate would syntactically demand the plural form (*"know"*). Instead, Aurora unmistakably employs the **third-person singular** (*"knows"*).
This construction is no casual oversight, but a brilliant psychoanalytic marker: the ego and the baby grammatically merge into a single, indivisible monad. Duality is erased. From two separate beings, a singular psychic construct is forged together. There is no longer an external other; the boundary between self and other has collapsed.

#### 20. The Hermetic "Only": Erecting a Fortress Against the World
The opening word "Only" marks the absolute exclusionary claim of this dyad. All other human beings, reality itself, and the outside world are radically locked out. The relationship seals itself off like a hermetic sect. This is the classic dynamic of a shared delusional world (*folie à deux*): the outside world must be declared ignorant, inadequate, or hostile so that the fragile internal illusion cannot be shattered by external reality testing.

#### 21. The Vacuity of Knowledge: The Absolute Predicate Without an Object
What exactly is it that "only me and my baby knows"? What stands out is that the verb "knows" stands entirely absolute. There is no direct accusative object, no subordinate clause introduced by "that", no substantive determination. It is pure, empty knowledge of a secret whose content does not exist. The ego claims privileged cognitive access in order to compensate for the fundamental non-knowledge of Track 04 ("Don't know why I do it"). Yet this knowledge remains a hollow shell: they know nothing, except that they are going in circles.

#### 22. The Eightfold Litany of the Bridge: Exhaustion Through Compulsive Repetition
The couplet "Round and round in circles we go / Only me and my baby knows" is executed no fewer than **eight consecutive times** (sixteen unbroken lines of text). This shatters all conventional pop dramaturgy. The bridge is no dynamic transition, but a hypnotic maelstrom. The sheer quantity of repetition exerts a sedating, wearisome effect on the listener: any critical interrogation is drowned in the monotonous regularity of the beat. The psyche hammers the dogma into itself until doubt is no longer conceivable.

#### 23. Phonation and Vocal Placement: The Infantile Falsetto as Camouflage
In her vocal delivery across this passage, Aurora does not deploy her powerful, incisive chest voice, but a conspicuously dialed-down, nearly vibrato-free head voice. Vocal placement is shifted forward into the hard palate; the tone is girlish, bright, and seemingly artless. It is the timbre of a child talking to itself in a playroom. This vocal aesthetic operates as psychic camouflage: by simulating infantile innocence, the subject evades adult responsibility and confrontation with existential crisis.

#### 24. The Pre-Chorus Echo: "I feel it, I feel it" Following the Vertigo
Emerging from the 16-line compulsive loop, "I feel it, I feel it" returns as a pre-chorus. Yet its inflection has subtly shifted: after the dizziness of circular rotation, the phrase no longer feels triumphant, but rushed, almost breathless. Feeling is no longer a serene resting within oneself, but a neurotic reflex. Following the dizzying spin of the bridge, the ego must frantically check whether the ground beneath its feet still holds.

#### 25. The Eroding Final Chorus: The Waning of Physical Substance
In the final chorus, the progressive exhaustion of the system becomes manifest: the baseline formula "My baby just cares for me" is no longer quoted four or three times, but contracts to a meager **two iterations**. The defensive narrative energy is spent. The mantra has lost its hypnotic power; it is reeled off mechanically and faintly, like a run-down music box whose mainspring is unwinding.

#### 26. The Final Suffocation Pattern: "I feel it, I feel it" as the Last Breath Before the Cut
The song ends not with a reassuring formula of care and emotional security, but with the fourfold repetition of "I feel it, I feel it / I feel it, I feel it". This is the dramaturgical final image: the ego clings to its own assertion with its fingernails. It talks itself into sensation until the track cuts off abruptly. There is no gentle fade-out into cozy warmth, but a hard cut into silence. The listener is left not with the sensation of fulfilled love, but with the suffocating aftertaste of an obsessive-compulsive neurosis whose energy source has run dry.

#### 27. The Dramaturgical Fracture Point: The Inevitable Collapse of the Dyad
Point 27 marks the inevitable tipping point: the closed dyadic world of "My Baby" fails on account of its own absolute hermeticism. Because the partner ("my baby") is in truth merely an imagined cardboard cutout, a transitional object devoid of an autonomous life, it cannot permanently rescue the ego from existential loneliness. The fusion ("Only me and my baby knows") is a chimera. When this fiction of togetherness collapses under its own weight, the psyche in the subsequent Track 06 ("Have You Seen Me Dance Alone?") is left with only one radical consequence: the other is expunged entirely from consciousness, the dyad is liquidated, and the ego steps naked and solipsistic into the empty space of the solo dance.

### IV. Synthesis: Dialectic of Form and Content & Dramaturgical Fracture Point

The formal and psychodynamic mastery of "My Baby" resides in the consummate, vertiginous dialectic between its buoyant surface and its pathogenic deep structure. On initial listening, the track seemingly mobilizes every resource capable of comforting the listener after the harrowing crisis of "Ring The Alarm": a supple tempo, warm synthesizer pads, the delicate sweetness of a quoted jazz classic, and the reassuring monotony of a repetitive hookline.

Yet when dissecting the anatomy of this song, the musical idyll reveals itself as a profoundly unstable defensive pact against collapse. Every formal parameter documents the desperate attempt at ego-stabilization through regression:
1. **The quotation as prosthesis:** The ego no longer speaks from its own authority, but borrows the words of Nina Simone and Gus Kahn to plaster over its own muteness.
2. **The degradation of desire:** By replacing "loves" with "cares" and qualifying it through the duplicitous "just", desire capitulates before the complexities of mature love and flees into the undemanding niche of mere custodial care.
3. **The decay of syntax:** From the complete subject-predicate construction ("My baby just cares for me") to the bare directional vector ("For me, for me") and the apocopated amputation ("My baby just cares for..."), down to the infantile stammering ("Me, Me / Me, Me"), the song enacts a clinically precise dismantling of language.
4. **The grammar of pathology:** In the line "Only me and my baby knows", language manifests the symptom of symbiosis: two subjects are welded by a singular verb into a closed monad that hysterically banishes any outside world ("Only").
5. **Circular somatics:** The eightfold repetition of the bridge ("Round and round in circles we go") translates mental paralysis into the motor stereotypy of a rocking motion (*jactatio corporis*), while the persistent "I feel it" does not articulate affect, but is coerced as an autosuggestive self-test against inner numbness.

**The Dramaturgical Fracture Point Leading into Track 06:**
"My Baby" carries the seed of its own destruction from the very beginning. A dyadic cult can survive only as long as the other complies without contradiction in their assigned role as a silent shield. Yet because this "baby" possesses no independent voice in the text, remaining a pure phantasm of the needy ego, the construct collapses under its own detachment from reality. The dizzying spin of circular motion cannot be sustained indefinitely; eventually, vertigo sets in, the music box winds down, and the artificially respirated "I feel it" expires in the hard cut of the outro.

Thus, the trajectory of the album's dramaturgy is sealed: the flight into dyadic fusion has failed. The ego has learned that even the tightest enmeshment cannot cure loneliness when the other is merely an invention of defensive denial. Therefore, in the subsequent Track 06 ("Have You Seen Me Dance Alone?"), there remains no alternative but to radically strip away the illusion of partnership. The ego liquidates the "baby", erases the "we", and flings itself into solipsistic self-assertion: if no one cares for me, there exists no one in this world but myself—and the dance in circles transforms into a solitary dance before an imaginary witness.
